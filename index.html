Someone you’ve never met is about to host your open house.

Porch Partner is for real estate agents who can’t make one of their own open houses. It finds a licensed agent nearby who can host it, gives that agent a reason to say yes, and gives the listing agent reasons to trust them. Below: who it’s for, the four decisions I made, and five screens you can try.

Two agents, and they’re mostly not the same person

The agent who needs cover and the agent who’d give it have different weekends and different problems. That shaped every decision below, starting with what the covering agent gets. One industry guide backs this split; I haven’t interviewed agents yet.

  • An answer fast, not a chat that goes quiet.
  • Someone who will turn up, and who the seller would be fine with.
  • No favor to repay later.
Covering agent

Priya Shah, Queen City Homes

Priya got licensed 14 months ago, has free weekends and not enough buyers. An open house is where buyers with no agent walk in. Priya would gladly host one, and has no way to find out who needs it.

  • To see what’s on offer before saying yes.
  • A way to earn the trust of listing agents who haven’t met Priya.
  • No awkward ask about who keeps the buyers.

Four decisions

Each one is a choice against something reasonable. The screens further down show them working.

  1. 1Who may cover

    Colleagues first, outside agents where the state allows it

    A coverage request goes to the listing agent’s own brokerage. The listing agent can widen it to any verified agent nearby only where the state lets an outside agent host, and only with the seller’s signed OK. The rules really do differ: North Carolina allows it, South Carolina doesn’t.

    Instead of any verified agent everywhere, or the same brokerage only.

    See it on screen 2

  2. 2What the covering agent gets

    Buyer leads by default, a fee when leads aren’t enough

    The covering agent keeps the visitors who have no agent of their own. The listing agent can add a flat fee to fill a request faster, and can switch leads off only if a fee is on, so a request always offers something. A fee moves brokerage to brokerage, never agent to agent.

    Instead of a flat fee only, or favor credits (cover one, get one covered). Credits need the same people on both sides, and they aren’t.

    Screen 2 and screen 3

  3. 3Why trust them

    A trust card: stars up front, checked facts inside

    The license is read from the state record every morning, not once at sign-up. After each open house both sides rate each other from 1 to 5 stars, and the two ratings appear together. The card shows the average, how many ratings are behind it, and whether the agent has covered for you or your colleagues. Inside, Porch Partner counts the track record itself: covers, on-time check-ins, late cancellations.

    Instead of a license checked once at sign-up, written reviews, or a hand-picked network.

    Screen 1 and screen 4

  4. 4How a match is made, and unmade

    Agents raise a hand, the listing agent picks, the rest are backups

    Nobody grabs a request and nobody is assigned one. The listing agent picks from trust cards. If the pick cancels, stops having an active license or doesn’t check in, a backup who already said yes is one step away.

    Instead of first to say yes gets it, or the service choosing the best match.

    Screen 4 and screen 5

What I’d build first

The brief names maps, calendars, license checks and money. I went deep on the license check and the trust around it, because that’s what makes a listing agent say yes to someone they’ve never met. The other three can wait.

Screen 1: Sign up and license check

Tom Reyes got licensed two months ago and wants to cover open houses. Tom types a license number, Porch Partner finds it on North Carolina’s state record, and a photo ID step checks that the name on Tom’s ID is the name on the license.

The decision. I don’t ask for a copy of the license. A copy shows what was true the day the card was issued, not today. Porch Partner reads the state record itself, every morning, from the file the Real Estate Commission updates daily and sells for $250 a year. A verified agent is one whose license is active in today’s file.

Why “active”, not “licensed”. North Carolina licenses everyone as a broker. Tom, two months in, is a provisional broker, and his license is active only while a broker-in-charge supervises him. An inactive license is still a license, but its holder has to stop working.

Why the photo ID. License numbers are public. Without a second check, anyone could sign up as Tom.

The tradeoff. Sign-up is a step longer. “Checked today” means this morning’s file, so a license that lapses at noon shows tomorrow. And the photo ID only matches a name: the state record has no photo or birth date to compare, so someone who shares Tom’s name could pass. If that turns out to be a real risk, I’d send a code to the email on the Commission’s record.

Try it: change what the state record says, in the dashed box.

Screen 2: Post a coverage request

Dana needs cover at 214 Elm St on Saturday. Two choices take up the screen: who can see the request, and what it offers. The bottom of the screen shows the request the way covering agents will see it, and it changes as Dana does.

Who sees it. The request starts inside Harbor Realty, with Dana’s 13 colleagues. Widening it to any verified agent nearby asks for one thing first: the seller’s signed OK, which North Carolina requires before an outside agent may host. In a state that doesn’t allow outside agents, the second option wouldn’t be there at all.

What it offers. Buyer leads are on by default, because that’s what the agents who’d cover already want. The fee is for when leads won’t be enough, like a quiet street. Leads can go off only when a fee is on, so no request offers nothing. A fee goes brokerage to brokerage.

The tradeoff. Defaulting to leads bets that a busy listing agent will give up the visitors with no agent. I haven’t tested that with agents; if I’m wrong, leads become a choice made on each request. Widening is by hand here, so a request can sit with colleagues who aren’t free. And that a fee must go through the broker is proved for South Carolina and Texas, not yet for North Carolina.

Try it: widen the request. Then, with the fee at $0, try to switch buyer leads off.

Screen 3: Find an open house to cover, and raise a hand

Priya has a free weekend. Each coverage request leads with its offer and with how many groups came last time, because that’s what tells Priya whether the buyer leads are worth an afternoon.

The incentive. 214 Elm St drew 11 groups last time, 4 with no agent: leads alone are a good offer. 9 Birch Ct drew 2, so Dana added $75. The offer is on the request before anything else, so nobody has to ask “what do I get?”.

Raising a hand. Priya isn’t taking the request. Priya is telling Dana “I’m free and willing”, and that shows Dana Priya’s trust card. An agent can raise a hand on several requests, but not on two at the same hour.

The tradeoff. First to say yes would be faster, and Priya would know at once. I chose raising a hand because the listing agent is handing over a client’s home and should choose who walks in. The cost is a wait, and sometimes not being picked. Priya hears either way.

Try it: raise a hand on 214 Elm St, then scroll to 33 Garnet Way. Pull the distance down to 1 mile.

Screen 4: Pick from trust cards, then rate

Three agents raised a hand for 214 Elm St. Dana has never met any of them. Each has a trust card. Closed, it shows a star rating, the number of ratings behind it, and who the agent has covered for. Open, every line says where it came from: Porch Partner’s own count, other listing agents, or the state record.

The decision. The closed card answers what one agent asks another about a stranger: how did they do, and has anyone I know used them. So it shows the stars, the number of ratings, and who they’ve covered for: you first, then your colleagues by name. I use stars because every agent reads them without being taught, and one line works for 7 ratings or 700. The facts Porch Partner counts are inside the card: covers, on-time check-ins, late cancellations. Dana can’t pick without opening it.

The license. It isn’t on the cards. Only an agent whose license is active in today’s state record can raise a hand, so the line would say the same thing three times. The screen says it once, above the cards, and each open card has the license number.

Picking. Dana picks one, and the others are asked to stay as backups in the order they raised a hand. Dana can mark colleagues and agents who’ve covered before, so their raised hand is picked at once.

On the day. The picked agent checks in at the house, by location, at least 15 minutes before the start. The lockbox code shows only then. Every group that visits goes on the sign-in list. The whole list goes to Dana; the groups with no agent are the covering agent’s buyer leads.

Rating. Afterwards each side gives the other 1 to 5 stars. Neither sees the other’s rating until both are in, so nobody rates to get even.

The tradeoff. Star averages creep up until everyone is a 4.8. That is shown for one online marketplace, not for agents. The count beside the stars and the late cancellations inside the card are what I’m relying on to keep a 4.8 honest. A card shows no average until it has 3 ratings, so a new agent’s card is nearly empty. I’d rather it say New than show 5.0 from one person. New agents get their first covers inside their own brokerage, where colleagues already know them. One late cancellation stays inside the card. A run of recent ones should go on the front, and I haven’t set that rule.

Try it: open each card and pick someone. Then use the dashed box to jump to after the open house, and rate.

Screen 5: When things go wrong

Four things I expect to go wrong most. In each one Dana’s phone says what happened, what Porch Partner has already done about it, and what’s left for Dana to decide.

The decision. Backups are the plan. Because agents raised a hand instead of one of them grabbing the request, someone who already said yes is waiting behind the pick. A cancellation, or a license that stops being active, hands the cover to the first backup without Dana doing anything. A no-show is different: with 15 minutes left Dana chooses, because only Dana knows whether a phone call will fix it.

What Porch Partner does

    24 hours, both ways. A covering agent who cancels with more than 24 hours to go loses nothing. Inside 24 hours it goes on their track record as a late cancellation, whatever the reason, because Porch Partner can’t check reasons. That counts a real emergency too. A listing agent who cancels inside 24 hours still owes any fee.

    What I haven’t designed. Damage to the home, safety at the door, and two agents disagreeing over who met a buyer first. I’m assuming cancellations and no-shows matter most. No agent has told me so.

    Try it: choose what goes wrong in the dashed box, then make Dana’s choice inside the phone.

    The ones I’d test first

    All unproved, and each would change a screen.

    • I’m assuming

      If I’m wrong

    • The brief means agents from other brokerages can cover, not only colleagues.

      Same brokerage only becomes enough, and most of the trust and license work shrinks.

    • Listing agents will give up the visitors with no agent, because they’re too busy to follow up.

      Buyer leads become a choice on each request, not the default.

    • A photo ID whose name matches the license is enough to tie an account to the licensed agent.

      Sign-up gains a step: a code sent to the email on the Commission’s record, or the agent’s broker-in-charge confirms.

    • Listing agents want to choose who covers their listing.

      First to say yes is faster and simpler, and screen 4 mostly goes.

    • Agents who raised a hand and weren’t picked will agree to wait as backups.

      A cancellation falls back to posting the request again.

    • The seller’s signed OK can also let the covering agent work with buyers met at the open house.

      For outside agents, leads need a second sign-off or the offer falls back to a fee.

    • Covering agents are fine with checking in by location.

      “On time” has to come from the listing agent’s say-so.

    • The number of ratings and the late cancellations inside the card keep a star average useful, even if averages creep up.

    Still open

    • How an outside agent’s fee is paid in North Carolina. South Carolina and Texas say only through the agent’s own broker; North Carolina I haven’t proved.
    • Whether the listing agent’s broker must agree to widening. North Carolina’s commission notes that a brokerage’s insurance often covers only its own agents.
    • Whether a request should widen by itself after a set time, not only by hand.
    • Whether colleagues at another branch of the same brokerage count as insiders in North Carolina.
    • Whether a provisional broker, North Carolina’s name for a new agent like Tom, may host for another brokerage.
    • Two I’d ask before building: whether “agents nearby” is meant to include other brokerages, and which state comes first.

    State rules, briefly

    Who may host an open house for another brokerage is a matter of state law, and the three states I read disagree. I’m not a lawyer and this isn’t legal advice.

    • North Carolina
      Allows it. An outside agent may host as the seller’s agent, with the seller’s written, signed permission.NC Real Estate Commission bulletin, April 2021 and NC REALTORS Legal Q&A, Aug 21, 2025
    • South Carolina
      Doesn’t allow it. An agent can’t host for another brokerage, or for another broker-in-charge inside the same brokerage.SC Real Estate Commission guidance, Nov 20, 2025
    • California
      No ban, but the regulator advises against it.CA Department of Real Estate, June 12, 2025
    • The other 47
      Unread. In Porch Partner each one starts closed to outside agents until someone has read its rules.
